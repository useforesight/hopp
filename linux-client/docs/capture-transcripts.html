How Capture writes a transcript

Capture turns each person's call audio into timed lines on this computer. It then shows those lines as messages, in the order things were said. This page follows a line from the microphone to the screen. It covers the changes of 15 October 2026 and the limits that remain.

What changed on 15 October

  • Words stay with the person who said them. Hopp places whole words, and it keeps each person's speech together when it batches audio for whisper.
  • Transcripts read in time order. Lines are still stored as transcription finishes. Hopp sorts them by start time when you read them.
  • One person's consecutive lines become one message. Nothing is reworded. The lines are joined with spaces.
  • Every speech line records when it ended. The capture database moved to schema version 2. Existing captures upgrade on first open.

The path from audio to transcript

The first six steps run during the call. Read runs whenever something opens the transcript, so it also applies to older captures.

TracksOne audio track per person.
UtterancesPauses cut each track into utterances.
BatchUtterances wait, then share one whisper pass.
Transcribehopp-whisper returns timed words.
Split backEach word goes to the utterance it was said in.
StoreOne line per utterance, in SQLite.
ReadSort by time, then join messages.

hopp capture follow and the MCP tool capture_follow skip Read. They return lines in the order they were stored, so an agent's cursor never skips a late line.

Each person is a separate track

Hopp knows who spoke from the audio track, not by guessing. Each remote participant arrives as their own LiveKit track. Your own voice comes from the microphone after echo cancellation. So every utterance already has a speaker before whisper hears it.

Times come from the audio itself. Each speaker has a clock that advances by the length of the audio received. If that clock falls more than 200 ms behind real time, for example after a network gap, it jumps forward to real time. Every time is in milliseconds since the capture started.

Pauses cut speech into utterances

Hopp listens to each track separately. It treats a frame as speech when the frame is loud enough compared with that track's background. These rules come from src/capture/vad.rs:

RuleValue
Counts as speech12 dB above the quietest level of the last 2 s, and above −50 dB
Ends an utterance700 ms of silence
Audio kept before speech starts200 ms, so first syllables aren't clipped
Silence kept at the end200 ms
Shortest utterance250 ms of speech. Shorter bursts are dropped as coughs and clicks.
Longest utterance20 s. Longer speech continues in a new utterance.

So a line's start time can be up to 0.2 s early, because of the audio kept before speech. Its end time includes the 0.2 s kept afterwards.

Natural pauses split one thought into several utterances. A sentence with a 1 s pause becomes two lines. Reading joins them back together.

Batches share one whisper pass

Whisper always processes a 30 s window. One 2 s utterance costs about as much as 28 s of speech. So Hopp queues utterances and sends several in one request. On LibriSpeech with large-v3-turbo, this used 2.5 times less GPU time than one utterance per request, with fewer word errors.

A request goes to whisper when any of these happens:

  • The waiting utterances fill 24 s, counting 1 s between them. A request holds at most 29 s.
  • The oldest utterance has waited 12 s.
  • The capture is stopping.

So text arrives in batches, up to about half a minute after it was said. That is the 12 s wait plus the time to transcribe.

Inside a request, each person's utterances now sit together. One second of silence separates a person's utterances. Three seconds separate one person from the next. The next section shows why.

Word timings split the batch back

hopp-whisper returns each token with a time. Whisper.cpp's DTW alignment works out that time by matching the text against the audio. Hopp then does three things:

  1. Joins tokens into whole words. Whisper writes "It's" as It + 's. A token without a leading space continues the word before it.
  2. Times each word by its last token with a letter or digit. Trailing punctuation is often timed late, so it doesn't count.
  3. Gives the word to the utterance that contains that time. Each utterance's span is widened by 0.5 s on both sides. A word outside every span goes to the nearest utterance.

Why the layout matters

When whisper writes nothing for an utterance, it times the next words early, into that utterance. This happens with a short "mm" or a word cut off. Before 15 October, people's utterances were interleaved in a request. An early word could land in someone else's slot.

Lines are stored as they finish

Reading sorts by time and joins messages

When something reads a transcript, Hopp sorts the lines by start time. It then joins one person's lines into a message when all of these hold:

  • Same speaker as the message so far.
  • Nothing comes between them. Another person speaking, an event such as a screen share, a bookmark, or a "not transcribed" gap each starts a new message.
  • The pause is 5 s or less. Hopp measures from the end of one line to the start of the next. The audio kept around each utterance makes the actual silence about 0.4 s longer.

The words are never changed. Hopp joins line texts with a space. A joined message lists the ids of its lines in joins, so you can trace it back.

Captures from before 15 October have no end times. For those, Hopp estimates each line's end at 400 ms per word, and at least 1 s. Their order and messages still work. Words that were split between people in those captures stay split, because the audio isn't kept.

Late lines during a call

The Captures window puts a late line where it was said. It re-sorts the call's lines whenever new ones arrive, then rebuilds the messages. A message can therefore grow, or split when someone else's late line lands inside it.

Agents following a call get raw lines in write order. capture_follow returns lines after a cursor, the id of the last line seen. A line that lands earlier in the call still has a higher id, so the agent never misses it.

How it was tested

hopp capture transcribe now accepts one recording per person. It plays them on one timeline, 100 ms at a time, the way call audio arrives:

XDG_DATA_HOME=/tmp/capture-test hopp capture transcribe Alice=alice.wav Bob=bob.wav

Use a separate XDG_DATA_HOME. Otherwise test calls go into your own capture history. The test tracks were built from LibriSpeech clips, whose words are known. The score counts each person's own words that are missing from their lines, whether lost or given to the other person.

TestInterleaved batchesGrouped batches
Short conversation (1 min, one interjection)Alice's "He" given to BobAll 85 of Alice's words hers
Harder conversation (3 min 47 s, 24 turns, 9 interjections)1.8% of words missing2.0% missing
Time to transcribe the harder one (large-v3, GPU)111 s (one run)116 to 140 s (three runs)

The missing words in the harder test are whisper mishearings, not misattributions. Examples: "an adam" for "and at 'em", "mental board" for "mantel board". The time difference is within the spread between runs. The machine's load average was 9.5 to 11 from other work at the time.

Known limits

  • Very short sounds often get no text. Whisper wrote nothing for Bob's 0.8 s "As for". "Mm" and "yeah" can disappear the same way.
  • Mishearings stay as heard. On a real call "Tuple" came out as "triple" and "Claude and Codex" as "cloud and codecs". Telling whisper the participants' names and a few product names may prevent some of these. That is untested.
  • Text arrives up to about 30 s late. That is the cost of batching.
  • Start times can be up to 0.2 s early, because of the audio kept before speech.
